Give an agent a memory and it will happily tell you things. The dangerous part
is *how* happily. Ask most "memory" layers where a fact came from and how much
to believe it, and you get a shrug: it was similar to your query, so here it is.

That's fine until two of your sources disagree, or one of them is a scraper that
guesses, or a user corrects something a system got wrong. Then a memory that
can't reason about *who said what, and how much to trust them here* doesn't stay
quiet — it confidently lies.

## Trust is not confidence

The usual move is to slap a single confidence number on a fact and call it done.
But confidence and trust answer different questions:

- **Confidence** — did the extractor read the text correctly? "The message says
  the plan is *growth*." A property of the *claim*.
- **Reputation** — how reliable is whoever made the claim, on this kind of
  claim? A property of the *source*.

A crisp, high-confidence extraction from a source that's usually wrong is still a
low-trust fact. Collapse the two into one number and you can't tell "the model
is unsure" from "the source is unreliable" — and those need opposite responses.

## Reputation is lopsided, so scope it

A source is rarely uniformly good. A broker nails `listing_price` and invents
`zoned_as`. A government registry is authoritative on `owner` and knows nothing
about how a tenant *feels*. One global score per source averages the sharp parts
away.

So reputation is **domain-scoped** — per (source, predicate). The lookup is a
simple ladder: use the source's score for *this kind of claim* if there's enough
history, else fall back to its overall track record, else stay neutral. The same
source can be trusted on one predicate and doubted on the next, which is how
reliability actually works.

## Snapshot the number, keep the history

Reputation changes as a source earns or loses credibility. The temptation is to
keep one live score and read it whenever you need it — which means every past
decision silently inherits today's opinion.

Instead, **snapshot** the reputation onto the fact at write time. Replay the
graph months later and you see the trust that actually drove the decision, not a
revised one. The score keeps evolving in an append-only history; the fact
remembers what it was trusted at. That's the difference between an auditable
memory and one that quietly rewrites its own past.

## Store the *because*, not just a verdict

When two claims compete, the winner shouldn't be a bare boolean. Alongside the
outcome, keep the decomposition: which dimension dominated — reputation,
recency, authority — by how much, and who the runner-up was. A fact that can
explain *why it won* is one you can debug; a fact that just asserts is one you
have to trust blindly, which is the whole problem again.

## Agreement should reinforce, not fight

Not every second claim is a conflict. When a *different* source asserts the same
value, that's corroboration, not a duel — the memory reinforces the incumbent
and counts the agreement instead of picking a fight. Three independent sources
saying the same thing is a stronger signal than any one of them, and that count
can lift the fact when it matters.

## The one-line version

> A fact isn't *true*. It's **claimed by a source, extracted from evidence, and
> trusted under context.**

Model that — reputation separate from confidence, scoped to the domain,
snapshotted with its reasons, reinforced by agreement — and your agent's memory
stops confidently lying. It starts telling you not just *what* it knows, but
*how much to bet on it, and why.*
